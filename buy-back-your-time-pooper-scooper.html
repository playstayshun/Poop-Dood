Hiring a Pooper Scooper: The Cheapest Way to Buy Back Your Time

🗓 July 31, 2026 ✍️ Corbin Key ⏱ 7 min read
Share:

It's 5:15 on a Tuesday. You just walked in after nine hours at work and a stop-and-go drive home, and somewhere between making dinner, helping with homework, and finally sitting down, someone still has to grab a bag or rake and deal with the yard. Most people, understandably, just don't want to. Work is stressful enough without adding a chore to the one part of the day that's supposed to be yours. Below is the actual math on how much of your year that chore is quietly eating up, and why paying someone else to do it might be one of the cheapest trades you'll ever make.

What an 8-to-5 Day Actually Costs You

A standard 8-to-5 job blocks off 9 hours of your day before you've done anything else. In practice, full time employees average about 8.4 hours of actual work on a typical weekday, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics 2024 American Time Use Survey. Add the commute, the U.S. Census Bureau puts the national average one-way commute at 27 minutes, or roughly 54 minutes round trip, and you've already spent close to 9.5 hours of your day before dinner is even on the table.

What's Actually Left?

About 5.7 hours a day for everything else — dinner, showers, kids, errands, and any actual downtime.

That last 5.7 hours isn't a free time bucket either. Cooking, eating, getting the kids to bed, and basic household upkeep eat into it fast. The BLS actually tracks what's left over after all of that: employed adults with no kids under 18 at home average 4.5 hours of leisure and sports time a day. Employed parents with a child under 6? Just 3 hours. That's the entire window you have to relax, connect with your family, or do literally anything that isn't an obligation — and it shrinks even further every time a chore gets added to it.

How Much Time Does Yard Duty Actually Take?

Here's where the math gets personal. If you've got one or two dogs and manage to stay on top of it, a realistic estimate is about 15–20 minutes each time you go out with a bag and a rake. Let it slide even a few days and that number climbs fast — more bending, more searching through grass, more bags. For a household that scoops twice a week at 20 minutes a session, that's nearly 35 hours a year!

4+ Full Workdays

3

Enough for a full binge of pretty much any show, at 30 minutes an episode.

4
A Full Week of Evenings

Spread across 5-hour family evenings, that's 7 entire nights handed back to you over the course of a year.

What It Actually Costs to Buy That Time Back

This is the part that tends to surprise people. PoopDood's once-a-week plan runs $50 a month for one dog — that's $600 a year. If doing it yourself once a week realistically takes about 30 minutes (a week's worth of buildup for one dog), that's 30 minutes × 52 weeks, or 26 hours a year handed back to you. Do the division and it comes out to about $23 an hour, less than most people already pay for a lot of conveniences they don't think twice about.

For households that want it handled twice a week, the math still holds up: $90 a month works out to $1,080 a year, buying back roughly 35 hours — about $31 an hour. That's squarely in the range of what a lot of people already pay for a house cleaner or a sitter for an hour, except this time you're not paying to have something done for you so much as you're paying to get an hour of your own evening back, every single week, all year long.

💡 The math in a nutshell: For about $1.65 a day — less than a single cup of coffee — a weekly PoopDood plan buys back roughly 30 minutes of your evening, every week, without touching your PTO, your weekend, or the little energy you have left after work.

What Would You Actually Do With the Time Back?

None of this matters much unless that time goes somewhere better than it was going before. Here's where people actually tend to spend it:

  • A family dinner that isn't rushed because someone still has to hit the yard before it gets dark
  • One more episode before bed, instead of chores after dinner
  • Real playtime with the kids — or the dog — instead of cleanup duty
  • A full session of the game you never seem to have time for anymore
  • 30 extra minutes of sleep, which most working parents could use
  • A few quiet minutes to actually decompress before the next thing on the list
